# EVIDENCE: new-onset type 1 diabetes in DKA (young adult woman)

Reference sheet for the builders and the clinical reviewer of `dka_newonset` (see `BRIEF.md`). It contains no patient numbers. It reuses the adult protocol in `../dka_inclass/EVIDENCE.md` and `../DKA_SERIES.md`, and adds what a new diagnosis needs.

**Sources and limits.** Clinical numbers follow the **2024 ADA/EASD/JBDS/AACE/DTS consensus "Hyperglycemic crises in adults with diabetes"**. Its full text could not be opened in the earlier session or this one, so details written from memory are marked **(verify)**. The instructor teaches from **Ignatavicius, Rebar & Heimgartner, *Medical-Surgical Nursing*, 11th ed.**, which could not be opened either. What this sheet says about that book is what its diabetes chapter usually stresses, and uncertain items are marked **(verify in Ignatavicius 11e)**. The 11th edition probably went to press before the June 2024 consensus came out, so it may still teach the older 2009 ADA numbers. Section 3 lists where the two differ so the instructor can choose. Glucose is in mg/dL and K in mEq/L.

## 1. Diagnosis and severity (2024 consensus)
- **D**: glucose 200 or higher, or a known history of diabetes. In new-onset disease the glucose is usually high (often 350-600) because no diabetes is known yet.
- **K**: beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) 3.0 mmol/L or higher (preferred), or urine ketones 2+ or higher.
- **A**: pH under 7.3 and/or bicarbonate under 18. The anion gap (Na - Cl - HCO3, normal 8-12) supports the diagnosis but is not part of the definition.
- **Severity** (verify): mild = pH 7.25-7.30 / HCO3 15-18; moderate = pH 7.00-7.24 / HCO3 10-14.9; severe = pH under 7.00 / HCO3 under 10, or stupor. Signs that call for step-down or ICU care: pH under 7.0, BHB over 6, K under 3.5 on arrival, low GCS, SBP under 90, HR over 100 or under 60.
- **Look for the precipitant.** In new-onset DKA the cause is the first insulin deficiency itself, sometimes with a viral illness on top. Also check for infection (CBC, UA, lactate) and get an ECG. **A pregnancy test (hCG) is required** for any woman who could be pregnant.
- **Classic history** (the cues in the textbook): weeks of polyuria, polydipsia and polyphagia, weight loss despite eating, fatigue and blurred vision. Then nausea, vomiting, belly pain, **Kussmaul respirations, fruity (acetone) breath**, dry mucosa, tachycardia, low BP and a slowed mental state.

## 2. Treatment summary (adult consensus plus the instructor's local rules)
- **Fluids**: isotonic crystalloid **1-1.5 L in the first hour** (0.9% NaCl or lactated Ringer's). Then **250-500 mL/h** guided by volume status (verify the range). Switch to 0.45% NaCl when the **corrected Na** is normal or high (verify the 2024 wording). Corrected Na = measured Na + 1.6 x (glucose - 100)/100. Lactated Ringer's carries 4 mEq/L of K, so count it.
- **Insulin**: **regular insulin IV, 0.1 unit/kg/h**. A priming bolus is optional (verify). Expect glucose to fall 50-75 mg/dL/h. IV insulin is a **high-alert drug**: double-check it, run it on a pump, and use a dedicated line or the port closest to the patient.
- **Dextrose**: at **glucose under 250**, add D5 or D10 ("two-bag" method) and either lower the insulin to 0.05 unit/kg/h or keep the fixed rate and add D10. Local protocol chooses. Keep glucose about 150-200 (verify) and **never stop the insulin while ketones persist**.
- **Potassium** (consensus, verify):

| K | Action |
|---|---|
| Under 3.5 | Hold insulin and replace K |
| 3.5-5.0 | Replace K to keep it about 4-5 |
| Over 5.0 | No K; recheck in 2 h |

  Give K only while she is making urine (about 0.5 mL/kg/h or more).
- **Local peripheral potassium rule (instructor; overrides the bag strengths above)**:
  - Peripheral K runs at **no more than 10 mEq/h in total**.
  - Give it as **KCl 10 mEq/100 mL riders, one at a time over 1 h, at most 4 in a row**, then recheck K.
  - A peripheral bag holds **no more than 20 mEq/L**. **40 mEq/L is the central-line cap.**
  - **Never stack** a rider on a K-containing carrier above 10 mEq/h.
  - Chart riders as IV med on the I&O.
- **Bicarbonate**: only if pH is under 7.0.
- **Phosphate**: only if under 1.0 mg/dL or with heart or breathing compromise. **Magnesium**: replace if low (common practice; not part of the consensus algorithm (verify)).
- **Monitoring**:
  - POC glucose every hour on the drip.
  - BMP, Mg, phos, VBG and BHB every 2-4 h, with the anion gap each time.
  - K 1-2 h after insulin starts.
  - Telemetry, and an ECG for abnormal K (flat T and U waves when low; peaked T when high).
  - Vitals hourly at first.
  - Strict I&O.
  - Neuro checks every 1-2 h. Cerebral edema is rare in adults but she is close to the under-20 risk age: watch for headache, vomiting that returns, drowsiness, bradycardia with rising BP.
  - Lungs after each liter of fluid.
- **Resolution (2024)**: **venous pH 7.3 or higher OR HCO3 18 or higher, AND BHB under 0.6** (verify). Glucose is no longer part of the definition.
- **Transition**: start once she is eating. Give **SC basal insulin at least 2 h before stopping the drip** (2024 says 2-4 h (verify)), and rapid-acting insulin with the first meal.
- **Starting dose for an insulin-naive patient**: **0.5-0.6 unit/kg/day**. About half is basal and half is mealtime insulin split over three meals, plus a correction scale (verify). Then check a BMP and BHB once for rebound ketosis.
- **Expect lows**: insulin needs often fall in the first days to weeks (the "honeymoon" from residual beta-cell function). One low glucose after transition is the expected teaching moment.

## 3. Textbook teaching vs the 2024 consensus (instructor's choice)
| Topic | 2024 consensus | What nursing textbooks commonly teach (verify in Ignatavicius 11e) |
|---|---|---|
| K threshold to hold or not start insulin | **K under 3.5** | Older ADA (2009) value **K under 3.3**; the book may say "do not give insulin until K is known / above 3.3" |
| Priming bolus | Optional; 0.1 unit/kg/h with no bolus is fine | Often an **IV bolus 0.1 unit/kg, then 0.1 unit/kg/h** |
| Bicarbonate | Only if pH under 7.0 | Same idea; some sources say **under 6.9** (2009). Emphasis that bicarbonate is not routine |
| When to add dextrose | Glucose **under 250** | Often **under 200-250** (2009 used 200) |
| Resolution criteria | pH 7.3+ or HCO3 18+, **and BHB under 0.6** | **Glucose under 200 + HCO3 15-18 or higher + pH over 7.3 + anion gap 12 or less** (2009 style) |
| First fluid | 0.9% NaCl **or balanced crystalloid** | Usually **0.9% NaCl**, then 0.45% |
| Glucose fall rate | 50-75 mg/dL/h | Some texts warn against a fall **over 100 mg/dL/h** (cerebral edema) |
| Overlap at transition | Basal at least 2 h before stopping (2-4 h, verify) | Often **1-2 h** overlap |
| Hypoglycemia treatment | 15 g fast carbohydrate (ADA Standards) | **15-20 g**, "rule of 15" |

Suggested approach: build the chart to the consensus, and add one Quick Check or teaching line wherever a student may have read the older number.

## 4. How the textbook frames DKA nursing care (verify in Ignatavicius 11e)
- **Framing**: the concept of **glucose regulation**, with fluid and electrolyte balance and acid-base balance as linked concepts. DKA is a medical emergency.
- **Priority problems**:
  1. Fluid volume deficit (hypovolemia).
  2. Potassium imbalance, with risk of dysrhythmia.
  3. Acidosis.
  4. Risk of hypoglycemia, hypokalemia and fluid overload during treatment.
  5. Knowledge gap about a new diagnosis.
- **Collaborative interventions** (in the book's usual order): restore fluids, give IV insulin, replace electrolytes, monitor, find and treat the cause, then teach.
- **Callout features** the book uses (titles may differ):
  - **Drug Alert**: IV insulin is regular insulin only, and it is a high-alert drug.
  - **Nursing Safety Priority: Action Alert / Critical Rescue**: check K before and during insulin; recognize the signs of hypoglycemia and cerebral edema and respond.
  - **NCLEX Examination Challenge** items.
- **NCSBN Clinical Judgment Model mapped to this patient**:
  - **Recognize cues**: polyuria/polydipsia history, weight loss, Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath, dry mucosa, tachycardia, glucose, pH, HCO3, gap, BHB, K, urine output.
  - **Analyze cues**: insulin deficiency leads to ketoacidosis, and osmotic diuresis leads to dehydration. The serum K can look normal or high while total body K is low. DKA, not HHS (HHS has little acidosis and very high osmolality).
  - **Prioritize hypotheses**: hypovolemia/perfusion, then the K shift on insulin (dysrhythmia), then acidosis, then hypoglycemia once on insulin.
  - **Generate solutions**: the fluid plan, K before insulin, the infusion and dextrose protocol, the monitoring schedule, the teaching plan.
  - **Take action**: hang fluids, confirm K, start or titrate the drip, give riders, add dextrose under 250, treat a low glucose, give basal before stopping the drip.
  - **Evaluate outcomes**: gap closing, BHB falling, K 4-5, urine output adequate, glucose 150-200 on the drip, mental status, teach-back.

## 5. Confirming type 1 at presentation
Adult-onset type 1 is common: about half of people with type 1 are diagnosed as adults (verify). Young adults are sometimes wrongly labeled type 2.
- **Islet autoantibodies** confirm autoimmune type 1. One positive antibody is enough, and about 90% of new cases have at least one (verify).
  - **GAD65 (GADA)** is the most common in adults.
  - **IA-2 (IA-2A)**.
  - **ZnT8 (ZnT8A)**.
  - **Insulin autoantibodies (IAA)** are useful **only before insulin is started**, or within about 10 days, because injected insulin makes antibodies (verify). Draw the antibodies with the first labs if possible.
- **C-peptide** reflects the insulin her own pancreas makes. **Low or undetectable C-peptide with a high glucose** supports type 1. Early on it can still be measurable (honeymoon), and glucotoxicity during DKA can lower it. A borderline value is repeated later. The ADA/EASD adult type 1 consensus advises a C-peptide test about 3 years or more after diagnosis when the type is still unclear (verify).
- **HbA1c**: 6.5% or higher meets the diagnosis of diabetes, and in new type 1 it is often over 10%, showing months of hyperglycemia. It is a baseline for follow-up (ADA target under 7% for most adults).
- **Differential if antibodies are negative**: ketosis-prone type 2, monogenic diabetes (MODY, especially with a strong family history), drug-induced diabetes (glucocorticoids, immune checkpoint inhibitors) and pancreatic disease.
- **Nurse role**: draw the labs before insulin when ordered, explain the tests in plain words, and tell her that type 1 is **autoimmune and not caused by anything she did**.

## 6. Screening for associated autoimmune disease (verify)
- ADA Standards recommend, **soon after the type 1 diagnosis**:
  - **TSH**, with or without TPO antibodies, for autoimmune thyroid disease. Repeat TSH every 1-2 years, or with symptoms. TSH drawn during acute illness can be misleading, so it is often done once she has recovered (verify).
  - **Celiac screening**: **tTG-IgA with a total IgA level**. Repeat with symptoms (diarrhea, unexplained lows, weight loss, anemia) or periodically (verify the interval for adults).
- Consider **vitamin B12** (pernicious anemia) when indicated, and **adrenal insufficiency** if there are unexplained lows with fatigue and low BP (rare).
- Other baselines follow later: lipids, a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and a dilated eye exam begin about 5 years after a type 1 diagnosis (verify). This is a teaching point, not an inpatient task.

## 7. Emotional response to a new chronic diagnosis
- **Expected reactions**: shock, disbelief or denial ("I'm 22, I'm healthy"), anger, fear of needles and of lows, grief for a lost "normal" life, guilt or blame, and worry about school, friends, drinking, dating, cost and the future. A young woman may also worry about pregnancy, body image and weight gain once insulin starts.
- **What the nurse does** (therapeutic communication, verify in Ignatavicius 11e):
  - Sit down, use open questions, name and accept her feelings, and give accurate reassurance (people with type 1 live full lives, finish school and have healthy pregnancies with planning).
  - Assess readiness to learn. Anxiety and the DKA itself shrink attention, so teach in short sessions, **survival skills only**, and repeat.
  - Give control early: she does her own fingerstick and her own pen injection under supervision.
  - Involve the support person she chooses (roommate, parent). Respect adult privacy and get her consent before sharing.
  - Watch for **diabetes distress, depression and anxiety** (screen with PHQ-2/PHQ-9 per policy) and refer to behavioral health, social work or a peer support group.
- **Later risks to mention to the team**: insulin omission for weight control (disordered eating) in young women; **alcohol** and hypoglycemia; driving safety.
- **School**: campus disability services can arrange accommodations (keep snacks and the meter in class and exams, extra time after a low). Teach the roommate the signs of a low and how to use glucagon.

## 8. Survival-skills teaching before discharge (new insulin user)
Use teach-back and return demonstration, and give written material in plain language. Full education continues as an outpatient (diabetes self-management education and support, DSMES).
- **Insulin pens**:
  - Basal insulin once daily at the same time. Rapid-acting insulin 0-15 min before meals, matched to carbohydrate later.
  - Pen steps:
    1. Wash hands and check the label, expiry and appearance.
    2. Wipe the seal and attach a **new pen needle** (4-5 mm).
    3. **Prime 2 units** until a drop shows.
    4. Dial the dose.
    5. Insert at **90 degrees** (no pinch needed with 4 mm needles in most adults).
    6. Press fully and **hold about 10 seconds** (verify against the product).
    7. Remove the needle into a **sharps container**.
  - Never share pens. Keep unopened pens in the fridge and the pen in use at room temperature for the product's limit (often 28 days (verify per product)). Do not freeze or overheat.
- **Injection sites**:
  - Abdomen, staying about 2 inches from the navel (fastest absorption).
  - Front and outer thighs.
  - Back of the upper arms.
  - Upper outer buttocks.
  - **Rotate within an area** and avoid lumpy spots (lipohypertrophy slows absorption).
- **Glucose meter**:
  - Wash and dry hands, use the side of the fingertip, apply blood to the strip and record the result. Know the meter's control check.
  - Check before meals, at bedtime, before driving and exercise, and when symptomatic.
  - ADA targets for most adults: 80-130 before meals, under 180 after meals. A **continuous glucose monitor (CGM)** is recommended for type 1 and is often started early (verify local practice).
- **Hypoglycemia**:
  - **Levels**: level 1 is under 70; level 2 is under 54; level 3 is severe, needing someone else's help.
  - **Signs**: shaky, sweaty, fast heartbeat, hungry, anxious or irritable, headache, blurred vision, confusion, and nightmares or night sweats.
  - **Rule of 15**:
    1. Take **15 g fast carbohydrate**: 4 oz (120 mL) juice or regular soda, 3-4 glucose tablets, or 1 tablespoon sugar or honey.
    2. **Recheck in 15 min** and repeat until 70 or higher.
    3. Then eat a snack or meal if the next meal is more than about 1 h away.
  - Avoid chocolate and other fatty foods (slow). Always carry fast sugar.
  - **Alcohol** can cause lows for hours: eat while drinking, never drink alone, and check before bed.
- **Glucagon** (prescribe for everyone on insulin):
  - Forms: nasal glucagon 3 mg, a ready-to-use injection 1 mg, or a 1 mg kit.
  - A **roommate or friend gives it** when she cannot swallow safely or is unconscious. Turn her on her side (vomiting), call 911, and give carbohydrate once she is awake and able to swallow.
- **Sick-day rules**:
  - **Never stop basal insulin.**
  - Check glucose and **ketones (blood or urine) every 2-4 h**.
  - Take correction insulin per her written plan.
  - Drink sugar-free fluids about every hour; if she is not eating, take 15 g of carbohydrate hourly as fluids or easy food.
  - **Go to the ED** for:
    - vomiting with no fluids kept down for more than about 2-4 h;
    - blood ketones 1.5 or higher that are not falling (3.0 or higher means go now), or moderate-large urine ketones;
    - glucose over 300 despite corrections;
    - belly pain, fast deep breathing or confusion.
  - Keep ketone strips at home (verify the thresholds against the diabetes team's plan).
- **Medical ID**: a bracelet or necklace reading "type 1 diabetes, on insulin", plus an emergency contact in her phone.
- **Nutrition basics**: consistent carbohydrate at first, then carbohydrate counting with the dietitian. No food is forbidden.
- **Follow-up**:
  - Endocrinology or diabetes clinic in about **1-2 weeks**, with phone contact sooner for dose changes (lows during the honeymoon).
  - A **DSMES / CDCES** referral and primary care.
  - Results of the antibodies, TSH and celiac screen.
  - Preconception counseling and reliable contraception (plan an A1c under 6.5% before pregnancy (verify)).

## 9. Discharge readiness criteria
This is a 24-hour course, so she will not be discharged inside the simulation. The criteria are for the Day 2 discussion and the Quick Checks. She is ready when:
- DKA has resolved and she is stable on SC basal-bolus insulin (about 12-24 h off the drip (verify local)), with no rebound ketosis.
- She is eating and drinking without vomiting.
- K, phosphate and magnesium are normal or replaced, and kidney function is at baseline.
- Glucose is mostly in range, with any low explained and the doses adjusted.
- **Return demonstration**: she has given her own pen injection and done her own glucose check (ideally more than once).
- **Teach-back** covers:
  - hypoglycemia signs and the rule of 15;
  - glucagon use (a support person taught too);
  - sick-day rules and when to go to the ED;
  - ketone testing;
  - insulin storage and sharps disposal.
- **Supplies in hand and affordable**:
  - basal and rapid-acting pens and pen needles;
  - meter, strips and lancets, or a CGM;
  - ketone strips;
  - glucagon;
  - a sharps container;
  - a medical ID plan.
- A follow-up appointment is booked with a number she can call; DSMES is referred.
- Emotional state is assessed: she expresses she can cope, has support and has had a mental-health referral if she screened positive.
