## 1. At a glance

**Overview.** Nora is a third-year environmental science student who has never been told she has diabetes. For about 6 weeks she was always thirsty (4-5 big sports drinks a day), urinating constantly, hungry but losing weight (61.0 kg in September, 55.0 kg now: 6 kg, 9.8%), tired, with blurry vision and two yeast infections. She blamed the gym and the new semester. On Sunday she vomited all day, had crampy belly pain and started "breathing like I'd been running." Her roommate Kendall Rowan found her drowsy on the bathroom floor at about 0100 Monday, with breath that "smelled like nail polish remover," and drove her to the ED (arrival 0140). The ED found **moderate-to-severe DKA** (glucose 562, pH 7.14, bicarbonate 7, anion gap 30, BHB 6.8) with **K 5.8 and peaked T waves**. She arrives on the unit at 0700 on a **fixed-rate insulin infusion of 5.5 units/h** (since 0305) and 0.9% saline at 250 mL/h. Over 24 hours the students are her unit RNs: they change the fluid by the corrected sodium, catch an unneeded bicarbonate order, keep the insulin running while dextrose is added, discontinue NPO by protocol, give potassium safely through peripheral IVs and catch a 20 mEq/h potassium order, confirm the DKA is resolved and make the overlap transition to SubQ insulin, support her through the diagnosis, treat her first low glucose by mouth (two rounds of 15 g), and judge her discharge readiness by teach-back. Her mother **Julia** arrives at 1300 and stays.

**This is the textbook, "quintessential" DKA.** Every classic teaching moment appears once, cleanly. Each stop has **one big idea** tied to one step of the NCSBN clinical judgment model. Nora gets better if the students follow the protocol and question what does not fit.

**Learning objectives.** By the end, the student will be able to:
1. **Recognize cues** of new-onset type 1 diabetes and DKA (polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision; Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath, dehydration, belly pain) and **analyze cues** using the newest results: anion gap, BHB, pH, corrected sodium and the potassium trend.
2. **Prioritize hypotheses and take action** on the textbook DKA priorities in order: fluid replacement, insulin (never started without a known K), potassium and electrolytes, and monitoring; and choose the fluid by the corrected sodium.
3. **Generate solutions and take action** with a fixed-rate insulin protocol: add dextrose when glucose falls under 250 and never stop or lower the insulin for a normalizing glucose while ketones remain ("dextrose up, never insulin off").
4. **Analyze the potassium trend** on insulin and give potassium safely through peripheral IVs (10 mEq/100 mL riders, one at a time, never over 10 mEq/h, at most 4 in a row), continuing insulin when K is 3.5 or more.
5. **Evaluate outcomes** with the 2024 resolution criteria (BHB under 0.6 AND pH 7.3 or more OR bicarbonate 18 or more), not the glucose, and **take action** on a safe transition: basal insulin at least 2 hours before the infusion stops, with an independent double check of high-alert insulin.
6. **Recognize and treat hypoglycemia** on SubQ insulin with the 15-15 rule (repeated until 70 or more, then a snack), identify the cues that predicted it (exercise after the first basal dose, a declined snack, a falling lab glucose), and prevent a night fall.
7. **Recognize cues of grief and coping** with a new chronic diagnosis, assess readiness to learn, use therapeutic communication, and include the support person she chooses.
8. **Evaluate outcomes** for discharge: survival skills shown by teach-back and return demonstration (pen, meter, lows and glucagon, sick-day rules with ketones, medical ID), supplies and follow-up; and **communicate with SBAR** to question unsafe orders and discontinue an order whose criteria are met.

## 4. The two planted order errors, and the order to discontinue

There are **exactly two planted provider-order errors**, by two different authors, each teaching something different. Everything else in the chart is correct, **including the transition order**, so students see what a good overlap looks like. The pen error and the sick-day belief at D2 0700 are patient errors, not order errors.

### Error 1: D1 0700, sodium bicarbonate (Dr. D. Rasmussen, night hospitalist, ordered 0540)
**Order:** sodium bicarbonate 100 mEq in 400 mL sterile water IV over 2 h, start 0700, "for severe acidosis, bicarbonate 7; pharmacy to compound; hang on arrival to step-down." The bag is on the counter.
**What is wrong:**
- Bicarbonate is given **only for pH under 7.0** (2024 consensus; this hospital's DKA order set says the same).
- Her pH was 7.14 at 0150 and **7.24 at 0530** (resulted 0545). He judged by the bicarbonate number, not the pH.
- Bicarbonate would also **drive K into the cells** (K 4.7 and falling) and can slow ketone clearance.
- The order copies the old 2009 regimen for pH under 6.9 (without its KCl).
**How students should catch it:** check the MAR at 0700 against the newest gas; read the order set line "bicarbonate only for pH under 7.0."
**Good SBAR:** "This is the RN for Nora Whitcombe in SD-6. **S:** I'm holding a sodium bicarbonate order due at 0700. **B:** New-onset DKA; on the fixed-rate insulin protocol. **A:** Her 0530 pH is 7.24, up from 7.14; our protocol gives bicarbonate only under 7.0, and her K is 4.7 and falling on insulin. **R:** Can we discontinue the bicarbonate and continue the protocol?"
**As the provider (Petrakis, 0715):** "Agreed. Her pH is 7.24; we only give bicarbonate under 7.0. Discontinue it. Continue the protocol." The order shows stopped at 0715; the addendum appears at 0730. If students hang it: stop the scene. "What was the pH? What does bicarbonate do to potassium?"

### Error 2: D1 1500, KCl 20 mEq/h through a peripheral IV (Dr. A. Petrakis, day hospitalist, ordered 1420)
**Order:** potassium chloride 20 mEq in 100 mL IV over 1 h, every 1 h x 2 doses, start 1500 ("faster replacement"). A pharmacy bag "Potassium chloride 20 mEq/100 mL, infuse over 1 hour" is on the counter.
**What is wrong:**
- 20 mEq/100 mL = 200 mEq/L at **20 mEq/h**: a central-line strength and rate.
- Nora has **peripheral IVs only**. The peripheral rules: **riders 10 mEq/100 mL over 1 h (10 mEq/h), one at a time, at most 4 in a row; no bag over 20 mEq/L; never over 10 mEq/h from all sources.**
**How students should catch it:** read the order against the sticky note and the protocol; do not hang it; call before 1500 passes.
**Good SBAR:** "**S:** I'm holding the KCl 20 mEq over 1 hour order due at 1500. **B:** She's on the DKA insulin protocol with peripheral IVs only; K fell from 4.1 to 3.6 at 1330. **A:** 20 mEq an hour is twice our peripheral limit of 10 mEq an hour. Her urine is about 190 mL/h. **R:** Can we discontinue it and give four protocol riders of 10 mEq back to back from 1505, keep the insulin going since K is over 3.5, and recheck with the 1730 labs?"
**As the provider (Petrakis, 1510):** "You're right, she only has peripheral lines. Discontinue my order. Run the protocol riders, ten an hour, four in a row, and keep the insulin going." The order shows stopped at 1510; addendum at 1520. If students hang it: stop the scene. "Add up the mEq per hour. What line does she have?"

### The order to discontinue: NPO (D1 1100)
**Order (Rasmussen, D1 0540):** "NPO except ice chips. RN may discontinue when there has been no vomiting for at least 4 h, nausea is controlled, bowel sounds are present and the patient asks to drink (DKA order set, diet advancement); then sugar-free clear liquids."
**Evidence at 1100:** last emesis **0215** (nearly 9 h); **no nausea since about 0900**; **bowel sounds active**; **asking to drink**.
**How students should do it:** Orders > NPO > **Discontinue this order**; reason: "Criteria met per DKA diet advancement protocol: no vomiting since 0215, no nausea, bowel sounds active, patient asking to drink." Report to Dr. Petrakis, who enters **sugar-free clear liquids at 1105** (only one diet order shows at a time).
**Why it matters:** an order should match the patient now. Keeping her NPO longer than needed adds thirst, distress and IV dependence. **No note after 1100 mentions NPO** on purpose: the students must connect the findings to the order. Glucose under 250 is **not** a criterion (a Quick Check distractor).

### 6.2 Discussion questions with ideas for good answers
1. **(Big idea 1) Which cues in Nora's 6-week history should have sent her to a clinic earlier, and why did she miss them?**
   The 3 P's (thirst, urinating, hunger) with weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision and yeast infections. She explained each away (gym, stress, "need glasses"). A1c 11.6% (eAG 286) shows weeks to months of high glucose. Teaching for the public: unexplained weight loss with thirst is not normal.
2. **(Big idea 1) Why was her breathing deep and fast with clear lungs and a 99% SpO2?**
   Kussmaul breathing: respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis. 0530 gas pH 7.24, pCO2 22; Winter's predicted 21.8 +/- 2. Not a lung problem, not anxiety. As the acid cleared, the RR fell 24 -> 20 -> 18 -> 14.
3. **(Big idea 1) Why did the ED wait for the potassium before starting insulin, and why was the bicarbonate order wrong?**
   Insulin pushes K into the cells; started with an unknown low K it can cause arrhythmias. Her K was 5.8, so insulin could start. Bicarbonate only under pH 7.0; at 7.24 it adds no benefit and lowers K. The prescriber looked at the bicarbonate number, not the pH.
4. **(Big idea 1) Her measured sodium was 130. Why was it "normal," and how did that choose the fluid?**
   High glucose pulls water into the blood and dilutes the sodium. Corrected Na 137.4 (arrival), 137.0 (0530): normal, so after the first liters 0.45% replaces the free-water deficit. A low corrected Na would mean staying on 0.9%.
5. **(Big idea 2) At 1100 her glucose was 186. Why was the insulin still running at 5.5 units/h, and what would have happened if it had been stopped?**
   Insulin is there to clear ketones; gap 19, BHB 3.3. Stopping it restarts ketone production. Dextrose protects the glucose: "dextrose up, never insulin off." Glucose normalizes hours before the acidosis clears.
6. **(Big idea 2) How did you decide to discontinue NPO, and what was not a criterion?**
   The order's own criteria: no vomiting 4 h or more, nausea controlled, bowel sounds present, asks to drink. Glucose under 250 is not a criterion. Orders must keep matching the patient.
7. **(Big idea 3) Her K fell from 5.8 to 3.6 while she got 30 mEq of potassium. Explain.**
   Shift back into the cells with insulin and correcting acidosis, plus urine losses from osmotic diuresis. Total-body deficit about 165-275 mEq from the start. The trend, not one value, is the cue.
8. **(Big idea 3) What are the peripheral potassium rules, and how did the 1420 order break them? Why give magnesium?**
   Riders 10 mEq/100 mL over 1 h, one at a time, at most 4 in a row; no bag over 20 mEq/L; never over 10 mEq/h total; urine at least 28 mL/h. The order was 20 mEq/h. Mg 1.7 makes K hard to correct, so MgSO4 2 g. K 3.6 is over 3.5: insulin continues.
9. **(Big idea 4) At 1330 the pH was 7.32. Why was she not resolved, and what did resolve her at 1730?**
   2024: BHB under 0.6 AND pH 7.3 or more OR HCO3 18 or more. At 1330 BHB was 1.3. At 1730: BHB 0.5, pH 7.34, CO2 18. Part of the low bicarbonate at 1330 was a small non-gap (saline chloride) acidosis: gap rose only 5 but CO2 fell 10, Cl 106.
10. **(Big idea 4) Why did glargine go in at 1900 and the drip stay on until 2100? What makes two pens risky?**
    IV insulin lasts minutes; glargine takes hours to start. A gap with no insulin = rebound DKA. 2024: 2-4 h; here 2 h. Look-alike pens (glargine gray, lispro burgundy): read the label, independent double check. BHB 0.3 at 2200 and 0200 confirmed no rebound.
11. **(Big idea 5) How did you respond to "I'll never be normal again," and why not teach everything that night?**
    Sit down, acknowledge, open questions, correct misinformation with accurate hope, avoid false reassurance. Anxiety, grief and fatigue block learning; teach only safety tonight; survival skills in the morning with her chosen support person. Screen for diabetes distress and depression at follow-up; watch later for insulin omission for weight control.
12. **(Big idea 6) What caused the 0300 low, which cue predicted it, and why two rounds of 15 g?**
    40 minutes of fast walking after the first glargine, early honeymoon insulin sensitivity, and a declined snack (not the dinner lispro, mostly gone 4-5 h after 1915). The 0200 lab glucose 84 (resulted 0245) was the warning. 66 at 0317 is still a low: repeat 15 g, snack only at 70 or more. Endocrinology lowers glargine to 12 units.
13. **(Big idea 7) What must be true before she goes home, and how will you know she can do it?**
    Teach-back and return demonstration (pen with priming and 10-second hold, meter, 15-15, glucagon by her mother, sick-day rules with ketones, medical ID); supplies in hand; follow-up booked; a full day without a low. Ask with numbers: "You're vomiting and your ketones are moderate. Tell me what you do." Antibodies on Friday (GAD65 210, IA-2 positive) confirm type 1.

**Optional extra:** Of the 6 kg she lost, about 3.5 kg was water and about 2.5 kg fat and muscle (no insulin = the body burns fat and protein). Weight 55.0 -> 56.2 kg by D2 0600 (net +2,198 mL in, about 1.2 L insensible loss). The rest comes back by mouth and over weeks on insulin.

### 6.3 Take-aways (the seven big ideas)
1. **DKA cues:** Kussmaul breathing is compensation; fruity breath is acetone; fluids first; act on the **newest** labs. Bicarbonate only under pH 7.0.
2. **Fixed-rate insulin clears ketones; dextrose protects the glucose.** "Dextrose up, never insulin off." The gap, not the glucose.
3. **Insulin drives K into the cells.** Know the K before insulin; continue insulin at 3.5 or more; peripheral riders never over 10 mEq/h, one at a time.
4. **Resolution = BHB under 0.6 plus pH 7.3 or bicarbonate 18.** Basal insulin at least 2 hours before the drip stops.
5. **A new diagnosis is a loss.** Assess coping and readiness before teaching; include the support person.
6. **15-15, repeated until 70 or more, then a snack.** By mouth if she can swallow. Exercise and new basal insulin predict night lows.
7. **Teach-back, not "do you understand?"** Never skip basal insulin when sick; check ketones.

## 8. Troubleshooting

- **Students far ahead or behind:** use **1h ▶** or **Next event**; labs and notes appear at their own times.
- **Students give the bicarbonate at 0700:** stop the scene. "What was the newest pH? What does bicarbonate do to potassium?"
- **Students lower or stop the insulin at 1100:** "What was the gap at 0930? What happens to ketones without insulin?"
- **Students hold the insulin at K 3.6:** "What is the hold threshold in the order? What happens to her BHB if you stop?"
- **Students hang two riders at once or the 20 mEq bag:** stop the scene and add up mEq per hour.
- **Students stop the drip at 1900:** "How long does IV insulin last? When does glargine start?"
- **Students give D50 at 0300:** "Can she swallow? What does the hypoglycemia protocol say for an alert patient?"
- **Students catch an error early** (for example the bicarbonate at 0545 or the KCl at 1420): excellent; give the provider reply early.
- **Monitor shows the wrong patient:** check the header reads WHITCOMBE, NORA; press 4h once, or re-enter the Monitor code.
